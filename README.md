# Combined effects of hypoxia and low temperature on recombination rates of *Drosophila melanogaster*

## Abstract:
Recombination is essential for genetic diversity, adaptation, and genome stability. While environmental factors like temperature and nutrition have been shown to influence recombination rates, combined effects of multiple stressors remains unclear. This study examines how cold stress and hypoxia interact in *Drosophila melanogaster* across multiple genetic backgrounds. Using a quadruple X-linked chromosome mutant line, recombination rates were measured in 50,209 males from 194 backcrosses under four conditions: control, low oxygen, low temperature, and combined stress across five genetic backgrounds. Eggs were partitioned based on when they were laid by females to explore temporal patterns over four time periods in the first eight days after mating. Temperature and genotype both significantly affected recombination rate, whereas oxygen concentration alone did not show a major effect. Overall, hypoxia reduced recombination, while cold temperature increased recombination rate. The combination of low temperature and hypoxia showed an overall increase in recombination, but the magnitude and direction varied significantly by genotype, with a significant genotype by environment by time point interaction. Importantly, the combination of hypoxia and low temperature was not additive, meaning that one could not predict the combined response from the individual stressors alone. When broken down by interval, there were several genotypes that showed significant changes in genetic map lengths across various treatments, with the low temperature and combined treatments having the most overall change, whereas other genotypes had minimal to no change. Despite significant changes in recombination rate, we did not see any differences in crossover interference across treatments and genotypes, apart from a minimal difference in a single interval between hypoxia versus low temperature. These findings highlight the complexity of genetic-environment interactions and emphasize the need to consider multiple stressors and genetic variation when predicting evolutionary responses in meiotic recombination rate.
